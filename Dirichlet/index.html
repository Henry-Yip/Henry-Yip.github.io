Kernels in Fourier Series: Dirichlet and Fejér

24 February 2023, revised 25 October 2026. Level: undergraduate.

This is an introduction to Fourier series, written while I was trying to understand the Dirichlet kernel from my Honours Analysis course. Parts of it are inspired by my notes from Honours Analysis and Introductory Waves.

If you are new to Fourier series

Where the Dirichlet kernel comes from

The Dirichlet kernel arises naturally from Fourier series. Recall that on \(-L \leq x \leq L\) we write

\[f(x) =\frac{1}{2} a_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n \cos \frac{n \pi x}{L}+b_n \sin \frac{n \pi x}{L}\right],\]

with

\[\begin{aligned} a_m &=\frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^L f(x) \cos \left(\frac{m \pi x}{L}\right) d x, \\ b_m &=\frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^L f(x)\sin \left(\frac{m \pi x}{L}\right) d x . \end{aligned}\]

From now on let \(f\) be \(2\pi\)-periodic, continuous, with a continuous first derivative, and take \(L = \pi\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{a_0}{2}&=\frac{1}{2 \pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)\, dx, \\ a_n&=\frac{1}{ \pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x) \cos(nx)\, d x, \\ b_n&=\frac{1}{ \pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x) \sin(nx)\, d x . \end{aligned}\]

Substituting these into the \(N\)-th partial sum and using \(\cos ny \cos nx+\sin ny \sin nx = \cos n(x-y)\),

\[\begin{aligned} f_N(x) & =\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^N\left(a_n \cos n x+b_n \sin n x\right) \\ & =\int_{-\pi}^\pi\Biggl[1+2 \sum_{n=1}^N \bigl(\cos n y \cos n x \\ &\qquad\qquad +\sin n y \sin n x\bigr)\Biggr] \frac{f(y)}{2 \pi}\, d y \\ &=\int_{-\pi}^\pi\left[1+2 \sum_{n=1}^N \cos n(x-y)\right] \frac{f(y)}{2 \pi}\, d y \\ & =\int_{-\pi}^\pi D_N(x-y)\, f(y)\, \frac{d y}{2 \pi}, \end{aligned}\]

which defines the Dirichlet kernel

\[D_N(\theta)=1+2 \sum_{n=1}^N \cos n \theta .\]

A closed form

Using Euler’s formula \(e^{i \theta}=\cos \theta+i \sin \theta\),

\[\begin{aligned} D_N(\theta)&=\sum_{k=-N}^N e^{i k \theta}=1+2 \sum_{k=1}^N \cos (k \theta) \\ &=\frac{\sin \left(\left(N+\tfrac12\right) \theta\right)}{\sin (\theta / 2)} . \end{aligned}\]

(Hint: sum the geometric series, then multiply the numerator and denominator by \(e^{-i\theta/2}\).)

Partial sums as convolutions

Because \(D_N\) is even, we can shift variables:

\[\begin{aligned} f_N(x) &=\int_{-\pi}^\pi D_N(y-x)\, f(y)\, \frac{d y}{2 \pi} \\ &=\int_{-\pi-x}^{\pi-x} D_N(\theta)\, f(x+\theta)\, \frac{d \theta}{2 \pi} . \end{aligned}\]

Since the integrand has period \(2\pi\), the interval of integration can be shifted back without changing the result:

\[f_N(x) = \int_{-\pi}^\pi D_N(\theta)\, f(x+\theta)\, \frac{d \theta}{2 \pi} .\]

The Fejér kernel

For the rest of the post it is convenient to use period \(1\), writing \(D_n(x)=\sum_{k=-n}^{n} e^{2\pi i k x} = \frac{\sin\left((2n+1)\pi x\right)}{\sin(\pi x)}\). The Fejér kernel is the average of the first \(N+1\) Dirichlet kernels:

\[F_N(x)=\frac{1}{N+1} \sum_{n=0}^N D_n(x) .\]

I will state without proof that

\[\begin{aligned} \sum_{n=0}^N D_n(x)&=\frac{1-\cos (2 \pi(N+1) x)}{2 \sin^2 (\pi x)} \\ &= \frac{\sin^2\left((N+1)\pi x\right)}{\sin^2(\pi x)} . \end{aligned}\]

Convolving with \(F_N\) gives the Cesàro means of the Fourier series, that is, the averages of its partial sums.

Approximate identities

A sequence of \(1\)-periodic functions \(k_n\) is called an approximate identity (or approximation to the identity) if

  1. \(k_n(x) \geq 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\);
  2. \(\displaystyle\int_{-1/2}^{1/2} k_n(t)\, d t=1\);
  3. for every \(0<\delta \le 1/2\), \(\displaystyle\int_{-\delta}^\delta k_n(t)\, d t \rightarrow 1\) as \(n \rightarrow \infty\).

The key theorem is that for every continuous \(1\)-periodic \(f\),

\[\sup _{x \in \mathbb{R}}\left|f * k_n(x)-f(x)\right| \rightarrow 0,\]

so convolution with an approximate identity converges uniformly to \(f\).

The reader can check that the Fejér kernel satisfies (1), (2) and (3), while the Dirichlet kernel fails (1) because it takes negative values. This is the heart of Fejér’s theorem: the Cesàro means of the Fourier series of any continuous periodic function converge uniformly to it, even though the partial sums themselves need not.

In short, both kernels approximate \(f\) by convolution. The Fejér kernel does it more gently: averaging smooths out the oscillations of the Dirichlet kernel, so it copes much better with rough behaviour in \(f\).
