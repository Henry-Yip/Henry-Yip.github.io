    Theorema Egregium: Why Every Flat Map Is Wrong

    June 4, 2024 2 minute read Undergraduate

    On this page

    • The Gaussian curvature of a sphere
    • The Gaussian curvature of a plane
    • Isometric surfaces
    • Conclusion
    • Exercises
    • Changes on 25 October 2026

    Gauss’s Theorema Egregium (“remarkable theorem”) states that the Gaussian curvature of a surface is intrinsic: it depends only on distances measured within the surface, so it is preserved by isometries. Equivalently, two surfaces with different Gaussian curvature cannot be isometric, even locally.

    The Gaussian curvature of a sphere

    By symmetry, a sphere of radius \(R\) has the same Gaussian curvature \(K\) at every point. Its surface area is \(4 \pi R^2\), so by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem,

    \[\chi(\Sigma) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma} K \, dA =\frac{1}{2 \pi } \cdot 4 \pi R^2 \cdot K = 2R^2 K,\]

    where \(\chi(\Sigma)\) is the Euler characteristic. A sphere has genus zero (no handles), so \(\chi = 2\); this is the same \(2\) as in Euler’s formula \(V - E + F = 2\) for polyhedra. Hence

    \[K=\frac{1}{R^2}.\]

    The Gaussian curvature of a plane

    A plane is flat: both principal curvatures vanish, so \(K = 0\). A sphere and a plane therefore have different Gaussian curvatures.

    Conclusion

    This is why every flat world map is wrong. A plane and a sphere have different Gaussian curvatures, so by the Theorema Egregium no map from (any part of) the Earth to a flat sheet can preserve distances. Every projection must compromise: it can preserve angles, or areas, but not both, and never distances.

    The familiar Mercator projection preserves angles (it is conformal) but badly distorts areas: regions far from the equator, such as Greenland and Europe, look far too big compared with equatorial regions. This is one reason the size of India and of Africa is often underestimated.

    Other projections make different compromises. The gnomonic projection maps great circles (the shortest paths on the sphere) to straight lines, which is useful for navigation, but it distorts shapes and areas heavily and can only show less than a hemisphere, so it is rarely used for world maps.
