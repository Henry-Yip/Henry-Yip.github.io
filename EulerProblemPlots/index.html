Problem of Two Fixed Centres (Part II)

9 July 2024, revised 11 November 2026. Level: undergraduate.

Part 6 of 11 of the research log of my 2024 summer project on the three-body problem, at the University of Edinburgh, supervised by Prof. Jenni Smillie.

Previous: Understanding and Visualising the Shape Sphere
Next: Sundman's Power Series Solution to the Three-Body Problem (Part I)

This post continues the problem of two fixed centres, introduced in part 4, by converting my simulated trajectories into the coordinates in which the theory is written.

From Cartesian to prolate spheroidal coordinates

Recall the prolate spheroidal coordinates \((R, \sigma)\), with the centres at distance \(b\) from the origin and \(\sigma \in [0, \pi]\):

\[x= \pm \sqrt{R^2-b^2}\, \sin \sigma, \qquad z=R \cos \sigma .\]

The sign of \(x\) is carried by the \(\pm\), so it is enough to consider \(x \geq 0\). Take \(b = 1\).

Substituting \(R = z/\cos\sigma\) into the first equation and squaring,

\[\begin{aligned} x^2 &= \sin^2 \sigma \left(\frac{z^2}{\cos^2 \sigma} - 1\right) = \frac{(z^2 - \cos^2 \sigma)\sin^2 \sigma}{\cos^2 \sigma} \\ &= \frac{(z^2 - 1 + \sin^2 \sigma)\sin^2 \sigma}{1 - \sin^2 \sigma}, \end{aligned}\]

which rearranges to

\[x^2 = \sin^2 \sigma \left(x^2 + z^2 - 1 + \sin^2 \sigma\right).\]

(This step divides by \(\cos\sigma\), so it fails on the line \(z = 0\), which has to be handled separately.) Setting \(u=\sin^2\sigma\) gives a quadratic,

\[u^2+(x^2+z^2-1)\,u-x^2=0,\]

whose non-negative root is

\[u = \frac{-(x^2 + z^2 - 1) + \sqrt{(x^2 + z^2 - 1)^2 + 4x^2}}{2} .\]

Then \(\sigma_1 = \arcsin\sqrt{u}\) lies in \([0, \pi/2]\). Since \(z = R\cos\sigma\) with \(R \ge 0\), this is the correct angle when \(z \ge 0\); when \(z<0\) the angle lies in \((\pi/2, \pi]\), and we take \(\sigma = \pi - \sigma_1\):

\[\sigma = \begin{cases} \sigma_1, & z \ge 0, \\ \pi - \sigma_1, & z < 0. \end{cases}\]

Given \(\sigma\), \(R = z/\cos\sigma\) follows immediately (or \(R = \sqrt{x^2/\sin^2\sigma + b^2}\) near \(z = 0\)).

Interpretation

The moving body never passes between the two centres. It stays around one of them, so \(S = \cos\sigma\) covers only part of the interval \([-1, 1]\), whereas the \(\operatorname{sn}\) solution quoted in part 4 sweeps the whole interval.

At the time I read this as a disagreement with the theory. It is not one. This run started from rest, and with two equal centres a body released from rest always stays on its own side: it is a satellite orbit, bound to a single centre. Part 11 derives this from the second constant of motion and finds the orbits that pass around both centres, which need an initial velocity.

Revised on 25 October and 11 November 2026. The discussion of which branch of \(\arcsin\) to take described \(\sigma\) in the range \(\pi/2 < \sigma < 3\pi/2\), but in these coordinates \(\sigma\) only runs from 0 to \(\pi\). The rule is now stated directly: \(\sigma = \sigma_1\) when \(z \ge 0\) and \(\sigma = \pi - \sigma_1\) when \(z < 0\). The last section originally took the limited range of \(S\) as a disagreement with the theory and suggested giving the fixed centres very large masses instead of holding them in place. Holding them fixed is correct, and the limited range is what a satellite orbit shows.
