The Discrete Fourier Transform and the FFT: A Worked Example

18 June 2024, revised 25 October 2026. Level: undergraduate.

This post explains the idea behind the fast Fourier transform (FFT) for a one-dimensional sequence, and then checks it against a direct calculation on a small example.

The radix-2 decimation-in-time FFT

For a sequence \(x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{N-1}\), the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) is

\[X_k=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1} x_m\, e^{-\frac{2 \pi i}{N} m k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1 .\]

Suppose \(N\) is even. Split the sum into even-indexed and odd-indexed terms:

\[\begin{aligned} X_k&=\sum_{m=0}^{N / 2-1} x_{2 m}\, e^{-\frac{2 \pi i }{N}(2 m) k} \\ &\quad+\sum_{m=0}^{N / 2-1} x_{2 m+1}\, e^{-\frac{2 \pi i}{N}(2 m+1) k}. \end{aligned}\]

Factoring \(e^{-2\pi i k/N}\) out of the second sum,

\[\begin{aligned} X_k&=\underbrace{\sum_{m=0}^{N / 2-1} x_{2 m}\, e^{-\frac{2 \pi i }{N / 2} m k}}_{E_k} \\ &\quad+e^{-\frac{2 \pi i}{N} k} \underbrace{\sum_{m=0}^{N / 2-1} x_{2 m+1}\, e^{-\frac{2 \pi i}{N / 2} m k}}_{O_k} \\ &=E_k+e^{-\frac{2 \pi i}{N} k}\, O_k , \end{aligned}\]

where \(E_k\) and \(O_k\) are the DFTs of the even- and odd-indexed subsequences, each of length \(N/2\).

Both \(E_k\) and \(O_k\) are periodic in \(k\) with period \(N/2\), and \(e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}\left(k + \frac N2\right)} = -e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}k}\). So for \(k = 0, \ldots, \frac N2 - 1\),

\[\begin{aligned} X_k & =E_k+e^{-\frac{2 \pi i}{N} k}\, O_k, \\ X_{k+\frac{N}{2}} & =E_k-e^{-\frac{2 \pi i}{N} k}\, O_k . \end{aligned}\]

A DFT of length \(N\) thus reduces to two DFTs of length \(N/2\) plus \(N/2\) multiplications by the “twiddle factors” \(e^{-2\pi i k/N}\). Applying the idea recursively when \(N\) is a power of \(2\) gives the FFT, with \(O(N\log N)\) operations instead of the \(O(N^2)\) of the direct sum.

Example: the DFT of \([1, 2, 3, 4]\)

Direct calculation

With \(N = 4\),

\[X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{3} x_n\, e^{-2\pi i \frac{kn}{4}}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, 3 .\]

For \(X_0\), every exponential is \(1\):

\[X_0 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10.\]

For \(X_1\):

\[\begin{aligned} X_1 &= 1 + 2 e^{-i \frac{\pi}{2}} + 3 e^{-i \pi} + 4 e^{-i \frac{3\pi}{2}} \\ &= 1 + 2(-i) + 3(-1) + 4(i) \\ &= -2 + 2i. \end{aligned}\]

For \(X_2\):

\[\begin{aligned} X_2 &= 1 + 2 e^{-i \pi} + 3 e^{-2\pi i} + 4 e^{-3\pi i} \\ &= 1 - 2 + 3 - 4 \\ &= -2. \end{aligned}\]

For \(X_3\):

\[\begin{aligned} X_3 &= 1 + 2 e^{-i \frac{3\pi}{2}} + 3 e^{-3\pi i} + 4 e^{-i \frac{9\pi}{2}} \\ &= 1 + 2(i) + 3(-1) + 4(-i) \\ &= -2 - 2i. \end{aligned}\]

So the DFT is

\[[10,\ -2+2i,\ -2,\ -2-2i].\]

Using the FFT

Split into even- and odd-indexed elements, \([x_0, x_2] = [1, 3]\) and \([x_1, x_3] = [2, 4]\), and take their length-2 DFTs:

\[\begin{aligned} E_0 &= 1 + 3 = 4, & E_1 &= 1 + 3e^{-i\pi} = -2, \\ O_0 &= 2 + 4 = 6, & O_1 &= 2 + 4e^{-i\pi} = -2 . \end{aligned}\]

Write \(W_N = e^{-2 \pi i/ N}\), so that

\[X_k = E_k + W_N^k\, O_k, \qquad X_{k+N/2} = E_k - W_N^k\, O_k .\]

For \(N = 4\),

\[W_4 = e^{-2\pi i / 4} = e^{-i\pi/2} = -i .\]

Therefore

\[\begin{aligned} X_0 &= E_0 + W_4^0\, O_0 = 4 + 6 = 10, \\ X_1 &= E_1 + W_4^1\, O_1 = -2 + (-i)(-2) = -2 + 2i, \\ X_2 &= E_0 - W_4^0\, O_0 = 4 - 6 = -2, \\ X_3 &= E_1 - W_4^1\, O_1 = -2 - (-i)(-2) = -2 - 2i, \end{aligned}\]

which agrees with the direct calculation:

\[[10,\ -2+2i,\ -2,\ -2-2i].\]

The saving is invisible for four points, but it grows quickly: the direct DFT of \(N = 2^{20}\) points needs about \(10^{12}\) complex multiplications, while the FFT needs about \(10^{7}\). This is what makes Fourier methods practical in applications such as image processing, where an image is convolved with a filter to blur it or detect edges.
