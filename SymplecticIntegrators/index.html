    This post explains what makes an integrator symplectic, and compares second-, third- and fourth-order symplectic schemes on the figure-eight orbit.

    What is a symplectic integrator?

    Start from Liouville’s theorem in Hamiltonian mechanics: the flow of a Hamiltonian system preserves phase-space volume. For one degree of freedom, a small region of the \((x, p)\) plane keeps the same area as it evolves, \(dA = dA'\):

    So evolving Newton’s equations forward by a finite time defines an area-preserving (more generally, symplectic) map of phase space. A symplectic integrator is a numerical method whose single step is itself a symplectic map.

    This matters for long simulations. A symplectic integrator does not conserve the energy exactly, but it exactly conserves a slightly modified “shadow” Hamiltonian, so the energy error stays bounded and oscillates instead of drifting. Non-symplectic methods such as the explicit Euler method and RK4 show a steady energy drift.

    Checking whether a scheme is symplectic

    For one degree of freedom, write a step of a linear scheme as

    \[\begin{pmatrix} x_{n+1} \\ p_{n+1} \end{pmatrix}=A\begin{pmatrix} x_n \\ p_n \end{pmatrix}.\]

    The scheme is symplectic if and only if \(\det A = 1\). For a nonlinear scheme the same test is applied to the Jacobian of the map, and in higher dimensions the condition becomes \(M^{T}JM = J\) for the Jacobian \(M\) and the standard symplectic matrix \(J\).

    Higher-order schemes

    A family of explicit symplectic schemes applies, for \(i = 1, \ldots, n\) within each time step \(\Delta t\), the updates

    \[\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_{i+1}&=\mathbf{v}_i+d_i\, \mathbf{a}\left(\mathbf{x}_i\right) \Delta t, \\ \mathbf{x}_{i+1}&=\mathbf{x}_i+c_i\, \mathbf{v}_{i+1}\, \Delta t, \end{aligned}\]

    with suitably chosen coefficients. A third-order scheme (Ruth) uses

    \[c = \left(1, -\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}\right), \qquad d = \left(-\tfrac{1}{24}, \tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{7}{24}\right),\]

    and a fourth-order scheme (Forest–Ruth) uses

    \[\begin{aligned} c &= \biggl(\frac{1}{2(2 - 2^{1/3})},\ \frac{1 - 2^{1/3}}{2(2 - 2^{1/3})}, \\ &\qquad \frac{1 - 2^{1/3}}{2(2 - 2^{1/3})},\ \frac{1}{2(2 - 2^{1/3})}\biggr), \\ d &= \left(\frac{1}{2 - 2^{1/3}},\ -\frac{2^{1/3}}{2 - 2^{1/3}},\ \frac{1}{2 - 2^{1/3}},\ 0\right). \end{aligned}\]

    Each sub-step is a “kick” followed by a “drift”, both symplectic maps, so their composition is symplectic too.

    Accuracy of the higher-order schemes

    I integrated the figure-eight orbit for \(50\,000\) steps with \(\Delta t = 0.01\). The energy deviations were:

    Scheme Order Energy deviation
    Velocity Verlet 2 \(5.9 \times 10^{-5}\)
    Ruth 3 \(4.1 \times 10^{-8}\)
    Forest–Ruth 4 \(1.9 \times 10^{-8}\)

    The higher-order schemes improve the energy conservation by more than three orders of magnitude.

    A remaining problem: momentum

    For pairwise forces like gravity, the total linear momentum should be conserved to round-off precision by all of these schemes. In my simulation it drifted: the error was small, but it grew steadily with time.
