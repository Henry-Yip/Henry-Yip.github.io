Perturbations in Quantum Mechanics

14 January 2023, revised 25 October 2026. Level: undergraduate.

This is the quantum companion to Perturbations in Classical Mechanics.

Setting up perturbation theory

Suppose a Hamiltonian \(\hat H\) cannot be diagonalised exactly, but is close to one that can, \(\hat H_0\). Then we can approximate its spectrum systematically. Write

\[\hat{H}=\hat{H}_0+\lambda\hat{H}',\]

where \(\hat H'\) is the perturbation and \(\lambda\) is a bookkeeping parameter that we set to \(1\) at the end.

For simplicity, assume the spectrum of \(\hat H_0\) is non-degenerate: each eigenvalue \(E_n^{(0)}\) has a one-dimensional eigenspace, spanned by \(\lvert n^{(0)}\rangle\).

We expand the exact eigenvalues and eigenstates as power series in \(\lambda\):

\[E_n=E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2 E_n^{(2)}+\cdots\]

and

\[\lvert n\rangle=\left|n^{(0)}\right\rangle+\lambda\left|n^{(1)}\right\rangle+\lambda^2\left|n^{(2)}\right\rangle+\cdots,\]

where the superscript \((j)\) labels the \(j\)-th order correction. (The states \(\lvert n\rangle\) written this way are not normalised in general.)

Substituting into \(\hat H\lvert n\rangle = E_n\lvert n\rangle\):

\[\begin{aligned} & \left(\hat{H}_0+\lambda \hat{H}^{\prime}\right) \\ & \quad\times\left(\left|n^{(0)}\right\rangle+\lambda\left|n^{(1)}\right\rangle+\lambda^2\left|n^{(2)}\right\rangle+\cdots\right) \\ & =\left(E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2 E_n^{(2)}+\cdots\right) \\ & \quad\times\left(\left|n^{(0)}\right\rangle+\lambda\left|n^{(1)}\right\rangle+\lambda^2\left|n^{(2)}\right\rangle+\cdots\right). \end{aligned}\]

First-order energy shift

Comparing the terms of order \(\lambda^1\) gives

\[\left(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)}\right)\left|n^{(1)}\right\rangle =\left(E_n^{(1)}-\hat{H}^{\prime}\right)\left|n^{(0)}\right\rangle .\]

Take the inner product with \(\langle k^{(0)}\rvert\). Since \(\hat H_0\) is Hermitian, \(\langle k^{(0)} \rvert \hat{H}_0 \lvert n^{(1)} \rangle = E_k^{(0)} \langle k^{(0)} \mid n^{(1)} \rangle\), so

\[\begin{aligned} &\left(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\right)\left\langle k^{(0)} \mid n^{(1)}\right\rangle \\ &\quad=E_n^{(1)} \delta_{k n}-\left\langle k^{(0)}\right|\hat{H}^{\prime}\left| n^{(0)}\right\rangle . \end{aligned}\]

Choosing \(k=n\) makes the left-hand side vanish, giving

\[E_n^{(1)}=\left\langle n^{(0)}\right|\hat{H}^{\prime}\left| n^{(0)}\right\rangle .\]

To first order, the shift in energy is the expectation value of the perturbation in the unperturbed state. (Choosing \(k \neq n\) instead gives the first-order correction to the state.)

A classic example

Consider a particle in an infinite square well on \(-a \le x \le a\), perturbed by

\[V(x)=V_0 \cos\left(\frac{\pi x}{2a}\right).\]

What is the first-order shift in the ground-state energy?

The unperturbed ground state is

\[\psi_1(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \cos\left(\frac{\pi x}{2a}\right),\]

so

\[\begin{aligned} \Delta E_1 &= \langle \psi_1 \rvert V \lvert \psi_1 \rangle = \int_{-a}^{a} \psi_1^*(x)\, V(x)\, \psi_1(x)\, dx \\ &=\frac{V_0}{a} \int_{-a}^{a} \cos^3 \left(\frac{\pi x}{2 a}\right) dx \\ &=\frac{2 V_0}{\pi} \int_{-\pi / 2}^{\pi / 2} \cos^3 \theta \, d\theta\\ &=\frac{8 V_0}{3 \pi}, \end{aligned}\]

using the substitution \(\theta = \pi x/2a\). Filling in the integral is left as an exercise.
