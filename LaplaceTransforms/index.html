    Evaluating Integrals with the Laplace Transform

    June 6, 2024 1 minute read Undergraduate

    From the archive (2024). Corrected in October 2026; see the note at the end.

    On this page

    • Turning the integral into a Laplace transform
    • Computing the transform
    • Exercise
    • Credits
    • Changes on 25 October 2026

    The Laplace transform is usually taught as a tool for solving linear differential equations, but it can also evaluate integrals. Here we use it to prove the classic result

    \[\int_0^{\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} \, dx=\frac{\pi}{2}.\]

    Turning the integral into a Laplace transform

    For a function \(g(t)\), the Laplace transform is

    \[\mathcal{L}\{g\}(s)=G(s)=\int_0^{\infty} e^{-s t}\, g(t)\, d t .\]

    Setting \(s=0\) formally gives \(\int_0^{\infty} g(t) \, d t\). So we take \(g(t)=\frac{\sin t}{t}\), compute its Laplace transform, and let \(s \to 0^+\).

    Computing the transform

    Recall

    \[\mathcal{L}\{\sin t\}(s)=\frac{1}{s^2+1},\]

    and the standard “division by \(t\)” rule: if \(F(s)\) is the Laplace transform of \(f(t)\), then

    \[\mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{f(t)}{t}\right\}(s)=\int_s^{\infty} F(u)\, d u .\]

    Combining the two,

    \[\mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{\sin t}{t}\right\}(s)=\int_s^{\infty} \frac{d u}{u^2+1} =\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan s .\]

    Letting \(s \to 0^+\) gives \(\frac{\pi}{2}\). Therefore

    \[\int_0^{\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} \, dx=\frac{\pi}{2}.\]

    (The integral converges only conditionally, so the limit \(s \to 0^+\) needs some care. Abel’s theorem for Laplace transforms justifies it.)
