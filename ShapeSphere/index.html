Understanding and Visualising the Shape Sphere

4 July 2024, revised 25 October 2026. Level: advanced.

Part 5 of 11 of the research log of my 2024 summer project on the three-body problem, at the University of Edinburgh, supervised by Prof. Jenni Smillie.

Previous: Problem of Two Fixed Centres (Part I)
Next: Problem of Two Fixed Centres (Part II)

This post introduces the shape sphere, a way to picture the motion of three bodies by keeping track only of the shape of the triangle they form.

Reducing the three-body problem

Reduction simplifies the three-body problem by removing degrees of freedom associated with symmetries.

Let the three bodies lie in the plane, identified with \(\mathbb{C}\). Before reduction, the configuration space is \(\mathbb{C}^3\), the positions of the three masses. By Galilean invariance we can fix the centre of mass at the origin, which reduces the configuration space to

\[X = \left\{ x = (x_1, x_2, x_3) \in \mathbb{C}^3 \;\middle|\; \sum_{i=1}^3 x_i = 0 \right\} \cong \mathbb{C}^2 .\]

Next come the rotations and reflections of the plane, which act by

\[\begin{aligned} R_\theta \cdot (x_1, x_2, x_3) &= (e^{i\theta} x_1, e^{i\theta} x_2, e^{i\theta} x_3), \\ S \cdot (x_1, x_2, x_3) &= (\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2, \bar{x}_3). \end{aligned}\]

Taking the quotient of \(X\) by the rotation group \(SO(2)\) gives a space homeomorphic to \(\mathbb{R}^3\), whose points are oriented congruence classes of triangles. Fixing the overall size as well, by setting the moment of inertia to \(I = 1\), leaves a 2-sphere whose points are oriented similarity classes of triangles: the shape sphere.

The maps

For equal masses, the Jacobi map \(\mathcal{J}: X \rightarrow \mathbb{C}^2\) is

\[\begin{aligned} \left(z_1, z_2\right)&=\mathcal{J}\left(x_1, x_2, x_3\right) \\ &=\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x_3-x_2),\ \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\bigl(x_1-\tfrac{1}{2}(x_2+x_3)\bigr)\Bigr), \end{aligned}\]

and the Hopf map \(\mathcal{K}: \mathbb{C}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}^3\) is

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}\left(z_1, z_2\right)&=\left(u_1,\ u_2+i u_3\right) \\ &=\left(\lvert z_1\rvert^2-\lvert z_2\rvert^2,\ 2 \bar{z}_1 z_2\right) . \end{aligned}\]

One checks that

\[\begin{aligned} \lvert\mathcal{K}\left(z_1, z_2\right)\rvert^2&=u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2 \\ &=\left(\lvert z_1\rvert^2+\lvert z_2\rvert^2\right)^2=I^2, \end{aligned}\]

where \(I = \lvert z_1\rvert^2 + \lvert z_2\rvert^2\) is the moment of inertia. So \(\mathcal{K} \circ \mathcal{J}\) sends the 3-sphere \(I = 1\) onto the unit 2-sphere in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and rotating the triangle (multiplying \((z_1, z_2)\) by \(e^{i\theta}\)) does not change the image. This is the Hopf fibration. The details are in Chenciner and Montgomery’s paper, A remarkable periodic solution of the three-body problem in the case of equal masses.
